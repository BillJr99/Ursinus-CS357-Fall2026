## POGIL Roles

This activity uses the **POGIL** (Process Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning) structure. Before beginning, assign one role to each group member:

| Role | Responsibility |
|------|---------------|
| **Manager** | Keeps the group on task, ensures everyone contributes, watches the clock |
| **Recorder** | Documents the group's answers and reasoning in writing |
| **Presenter** | Speaks for the group during class discussion, summarizes findings |
| **Reflector** | Monitors group process, notes what is working and what is not, leads the Reflection section |

> Rotate roles across activities so everyone practices each one.

## Key Concepts

| Term | Plain-English Definition | Example You'll See Today |
|------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **AI tutor** | An AI system that provides personalized, on-demand explanations and feedback — like having a knowledgeable study partner available at 3am | Khanmigo answers a student's question about quadratic equations with a step-by-step explanation tailored to that student's previous answers |
| **AI detector** | Software that tries to identify whether a piece of writing was generated by an AI, typically by measuring statistical properties of the text | Turnitin's AI detection tool flags text with low "perplexity" (very predictable word choices) as potentially AI-generated |
| **False positive rate** | The percentage of innocent cases incorrectly flagged as violations — in AI detection, the percentage of human-written submissions incorrectly labeled as AI-generated | A 5% false positive rate means 1-2 students in a class of 30 will be falsely accused of using AI on every single assignment |
| **Perplexity** | A measure of how "surprising" text is — human writing tends to be more varied and unpredictable (high perplexity); AI output tends to be smoother and more predictable (lower perplexity) | Non-native English speakers often use more formulaic, lower-perplexity sentence structures, causing detectors to incorrectly flag their writing as AI-generated |
| **Bloom's Taxonomy** | A framework ranking cognitive tasks from lower-order (remembering, understanding) to higher-order (analyzing, evaluating, creating) | AI can now perform tasks at all levels of Bloom's Taxonomy, which forces us to rethink what education should develop in students |
| **Metacognition** | Thinking about your own thinking — knowing what you know, knowing what you don't know, and being able to monitor and adjust your own learning strategies | A student who realizes "I can follow this code when I read it but I couldn't write it from scratch" is exercising metacognition about their programming skill |

## Model 1: What AI Is Changing in Education

AI can be a 24/7 personal tutor or a homework vending machine — and which one it becomes depends almost entirely on how you choose to use it. A personal tutor asks you questions, points out where your reasoning is weak, and makes you do the work of thinking. A vending machine gives you the answer and lets you walk away. The technology is the same; the difference is in your relationship to it. This activity is about understanding that difference clearly enough to make it a deliberate choice rather than an accident.

AI is entering education in at least three distinct functional roles. Each role creates opportunities and introduces risks that are not always symmetrical — they often benefit different stakeholders.

**The Three Roles of AI in Education**

| Role | Opportunity It Creates | Risk It Creates | Example Tool | Who Benefits | Who May Be Harmed |
|------|----------------------|-----------------|--------------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Tutor** | 24/7 personalized explanations at any pace, immediate feedback on misconceptions, adaptive difficulty that matches the student's level | Over-reliance that prevents students from developing independent problem-solving; shallow engagement where students accept AI explanations without wrestling with the ideas; equity gaps where access depends on device and internet quality | Khanmigo (Khan Academy's AI tutor), Synthesis, Duolingo Max | Students with reliable devices, fast internet, and enough digital literacy to use the tools critically | Students without devices, connectivity, or a home environment conducive to self-directed AI-assisted learning |
| **Content Generator** | Rapid lesson plan drafting in minutes rather than hours, differentiated materials for different reading levels at scale, quiz generation on any topic | Homogenization of curriculum as all teachers use similar AI-generated materials; reduced teacher agency as AI suggestions crowd out professional judgment; low-quality outputs that require significant teacher editing to be useful | Diffit (text leveling), MagicSchool AI, ChatGPT | Teachers with very high workloads who need to produce materials quickly | Students who need locally relevant, culturally responsive content that generic AI tools do not produce |
| **Evaluator** | Immediate writing feedback that students can act on before submitting, scalable assessment at very low cost compared to human grading | False positives that accuse innocent students; systematic bias against non-native English speakers whose writing style resembles AI output; "teaching to the detector" where students optimize for passing detection rather than writing well | Turnitin AI detection, GPTZero, Winston AI | Administrators and instructors seeking to scale oversight without scaling staff | Students flagged incorrectly; non-native speakers; students who lose their authentic voice trying to avoid detection |

### Critical Thinking Questions

**Question 1.** Personalized AI tutoring could theoretically help students in under-resourced schools receive the kind of individual attention previously available only to students with private tutors. What infrastructure would need to exist before this benefit could actually reach those students?

*Hint:* Work through the prerequisites layer by layer. First, the student needs a device — is it shared with siblings? Available only at school? Then reliable internet — does it work at home after school hours? Then basic digital literacy — can the student navigate the tool, ask follow-up questions, and recognize when the AI is wrong? Then teacher training — can the teacher integrate AI tutoring into class in a way that supports rather than replaces their instruction? Who pays for each layer, and at what scale?

**Question 2.** If AI can generate lesson plans, differentiated worksheets, and quiz questions on demand, what does the teacher's role become? Does this represent a reduction of the teacher's professional value, or a transformation of it?

*Hint:* Think about what a skilled teacher does that a content generator cannot: noticing that a specific student is confused for a reason that doesn't show up in their submitted work; building a classroom culture where students feel safe to make mistakes; adjusting in real time when a lesson isn't landing; maintaining relationships with students across years. How does this parallel other automation transitions — like spreadsheets replacing manual bookkeeping but not the accountant who interprets the numbers?

**Question 3.** Look at the "Who Benefits / Who May Be Harmed" column of the table. Notice that the same tool often helps one group while disadvantaging another. Who currently has meaningful access to high-quality AI tutoring tools, and who does not? What would equitable access require?

*Hint:* Khanmigo is available for free to teachers and $4/month for students. GPT-4 access requires a paid subscription ($20/month as of 2024). What does the free-tier vs. paid-tier divide mean for educational equity? A student at a well-funded suburban school might use GPT-4 for tutoring; a student in an underfunded urban or rural school might have no access. Is AI tutoring widening or narrowing the educational opportunity gap?

## Model 2: Academic Integrity in the Age of LLMs

### The Detection Arms Race

AI writing detectors attempt to identify AI-generated text by measuring statistical properties (token probability distributions, perplexity, burstiness). However, this has produced an escalating arms race:

- **AI detectors** (GPTZero, Turnitin AI, Winston AI) flag text with low perplexity and uniform sentence structure — the statistical signature of most LLM output
- **Paraphrasing tools** (QuillBot, manual rewriting, style transfer prompts) restructure AI output to evade detectors by increasing surface variation while keeping the underlying content
- **False positive problem**: legitimate human writing — especially by non-native English speakers, writers using formal register, or students writing in constrained academic style — is flagged as AI-generated at alarming rates

A 5% false positive rate sounds small. It is not small when applied to a classroom. In a class of 30 students, you expect 1-2 innocent students to be falsely accused on *every* assignment. Over a semester with 10 assignments, that means every student in the class has a meaningful chance of being incorrectly flagged at least once.

### Rethinking Assessment

Rather than investing in detection (which is losing the arms race), many educators argue the better design move is to create AI-resistant assessments — or assessments where AI assistance is part of the assignment, explicitly governed.

| Assessment Type | AI Can Do It? | AI-Resistant Variant | What the Resistant Variant Actually Measures |
|----------------|---------------|---------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Take-home essay | Yes, easily — paste the prompt and get a polished response | In-class oral defense of the essay — student must answer follow-up questions about their argument in real time | Depth of understanding, ability to reason aloud under pressure, whether the student actually knows what they submitted |
| Multiple choice quiz | Yes, often — LLMs score well on factual MCQ | Explanation of wrong answers or think-aloud recording — student explains why the distractors are wrong | Conceptual clarity about why incorrect options are incorrect, not just pattern matching on the right answer |
| Code submission | Yes — LLMs generate working code for most standard problems | Live code walkthrough and modification under observation — student must explain and extend the code in real time | Ability to read, explain the logic of, and modify code — not just produce output that passes tests |
| Lab report | Partially — structure and prose are easy; data analysis requires data | Process log with timestamped notes and documentation of failed attempts, submitted alongside the final report | Scientific reasoning and experimental process, not just clean final results |
| Research paper | Yes — LLMs can produce structured academic arguments | Annotated bibliography with recorded literature discussion — student explains source selection and synthesis choices | Source evaluation, synthesis judgment, ability to explain why each source was chosen and what it contributes |

> ⚠️ **Common Misconception:** Many students (and some instructors) assume that "AI detection = proof of AI use" and "passing detection = proof of human writing." Neither is true. AI detectors have significant false positive rates (accusing humans) and significant false negative rates (missing AI output that has been lightly edited). Using detection results as conclusive evidence — in either direction — is not supported by the tools' own accuracy claims. Detection results are evidence to investigate, not verdicts to enforce.

### Critical Thinking Questions

**Question 4.** A class of 30 students is assessed using an AI detector with a published 5% false positive rate. In expectation, how many innocent students will be flagged? What are the consequences for those students, and how should an instructor respond to a positive detection result?

*Hint:* Compute: 30 × 0.05 = 1.5 students expected to be falsely accused per assignment. What does it feel like to be that student — to receive an academic integrity accusation for something you did not do? What burden of proof applies in your institution's academic integrity process? Does "the detector said so" meet that burden? What would a fair response look like that takes both the detection result and the false positive rate seriously?

**Question 5.** Research has shown that AI detectors disproportionately flag writing by non-native English speakers. Why might this be? What does this mean for using AI detectors in a diverse classroom or in international contexts?

*Hint:* Non-native English speakers often use more formulaic, lower-perplexity sentence structures as a learned safety strategy — avoiding complex constructions they are uncertain about in favor of simpler, more predictable phrasing. AI detectors are calibrated primarily on native English corpora. What statistical property is being measured (perplexity), and who does the calibration systematically disadvantage? If your classroom has international students, what is the expected false positive rate for that subgroup vs. native speakers?

**Question 6.** Design an assessment for this course (CS357) that could not be completed on a student's behalf by an AI, even a very capable one. Be specific: what does the student have to do, what does the AI lack, and what would you as an instructor actually be measuring?

*Hint:* The most AI-resistant assessments share a common feature: they require the student to be present and observable, responding to novel inputs in real time. A live debugging session with a specific bug introduced into the student's own code, that the student has never seen before, and that requires explaining each debugging step aloud — an AI cannot do that for you without you being present to relay it. What other assessments share this property? What specifically can you not outsource to an AI?

### Multiple Choice Question

A student uses an LLM to brainstorm a list of possible thesis arguments for a paper, selects one they find compelling, and then writes all the prose themselves without copying any AI-generated sentences. Under most current academic integrity policies, this is:

[( )] Always plagiarism because AI was involved at any point in the process
[( )] Always acceptable because no text was copied from the AI
[(X)] Potentially acceptable or not depending on the specific policy, the instructor's guidance, and whether disclosure was required — illustrating why blanket "no AI" or "AI is fine" policies are insufficient
[( )] Always acceptable as long as the student discloses AI use somewhere in the submission, regardless of what the specific course policy says

## Model 3: Designing for Learning, Not Just Output

### Bloom's Taxonomy Revisited Under AI

Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) arranges cognitive tasks from lower-order to higher-order:

**Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create**

A common argument is: "AI can handle the lower levels, so education should focus on the higher levels." But this argument collapses quickly on inspection: current LLMs can Analyze, Evaluate, and Create competently in many domains. What does that leave?

The answer may lie not in which cognitive level, but in **what the cognitive work is about**:

- **Metacognition**: thinking about your own thinking, knowing what you don't know — an LLM has no self-model of its own uncertainty that is reliably calibrated
- **Judgment under uncertainty**: knowing when to trust AI output and when to verify it — this is a human skill because it requires knowing when you, the human, can catch an error
- **Personal meaning-making**: connecting content to your own experience in ways that require your specific history, not a token predictor trained on someone else's text
- **Ethical reasoning in context**: applying values to specific situations with real stakes, where the right answer depends on who is affected and how

### The Metacognitive Layer

Learning to use AI well is itself a skill — and it is increasingly a professional one. This includes:

1. **Prompting**: articulating what you want precisely enough to get useful output — this requires knowing your goal clearly enough to specify it
2. **Evaluating AI output**: knowing when the model is hallucinating, oversimplifying, or confidently wrong — this requires domain knowledge and critical reading skills
3. **Knowing when to trust vs. verify**: domain-specific calibration of AI reliability — different domains have very different AI error rates
4. **Iteration**: refining based on AI feedback, not just accepting first output — treating the AI as a collaborator, not an oracle

| Metacognitive Skill | What It Looks Like in Practice | Why AI Cannot Replace It |
|--------------------|---------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Knowing what you don't know | Noticing that you can read AI-generated code without being able to write it from scratch — and flagging that as a gap | The AI does not know what your mental model is or where the gap between reading comprehension and generative ability lies |
| Calibrating AI reliability | Knowing that LLMs are much more reliable for explaining sorting algorithms than for explaining recent legal precedents | Requires domain knowledge — you need to know enough to recognize the error |
| Recognizing when AI is wrong | Catching a hallucinated citation or an incorrect formula before using it | Requires verifying against external sources — the AI cannot verify itself reliably |
| Personal synthesis | Connecting a concept to your own debugging experience in a way that makes it stick | Requires your specific experiences, not averaged-out internet text |

### Critical Thinking Questions

**Question 7.** Take one of the written assignments from this course (or a CS course you have taken). Redesign it so that AI assistance does not trivialize the assessment — but do not simply ban AI. Instead, design the assignment so that a student who uses AI well and critically learns more than one who does not use it at all.

*Hint:* Think about what happens when a student submits an AI draft, then has to: (1) identify three specific places where the AI was oversimplified or wrong; (2) rewrite those sections with their own reasoning; (3) add a paragraph explaining what they learned by finding the errors. A student who used AI well would learn more from this assignment than a student who wrote everything from scratch and never encountered the AI's errors. What assignment structure creates that dynamic?

**Question 8.** If "using AI well" is a learning outcome, what would you put on a rubric for it? What behaviors distinguish a student who uses AI as a shortcut from one who uses it as a thinking partner?

*Hint:* Consider rubric dimensions like: (1) prompt specificity — does the student's prompt reveal that they understood the problem before asking AI for help? (2) critical evaluation of AI output — did the student catch and correct errors? (3) evidence of iteration — did the student refine the output, or accept the first response? (4) integration of personal judgment — are the student's own insights present in the final work, or is it indistinguishable from AI output? (5) disclosure — does the student clearly document how they used AI?

**Question 9.** This course's AI use policies were written at a particular moment in time. How should they evolve? Who should be involved in revising them, and on what timeline?

*Hint:* Consider who has standing in this conversation: students who are directly affected by the policies; instructors who must implement and enforce them; the department and institution whose academic standards they reflect; accreditation bodies that set minimum standards; industry partners who will hire graduates; and the AI companies whose tools are being regulated. These groups have different interests and different amounts of institutional power. What process would produce a policy that is both fair and effective? And how often should it be revised given how fast AI capabilities are changing?

## Exercises

**Exercise 1: Policy Gap Analysis**

*What to do:* Locate the academic integrity policy for this course (in the syllabus or course handbook). Read it carefully. Identify **two scenarios** it does not clearly cover — situations where a reasonable student could be uncertain whether AI use is permitted. Propose specific policy language that would fill each gap without being either too permissive or too restrictive.

*Starter hint:* Look for gaps in the policy around: using AI to generate ideas or outlines (not final text), using AI to check grammar or spelling (not content), using AI to explain concepts you then apply yourself, using AI to generate starter code you then modify substantially, and using AI to find sources you then read and cite yourself. For each gap you find, draft proposed policy language in the style of the existing policy — specific enough to give students clear guidance, not so restrictive that legitimate educational AI use is prohibited.

*You've succeeded when:* You have two scenario descriptions (specific enough that a student could recognize their own situation), two proposed policy additions written in formal policy language, and a brief explanation of why the current policy fails to cover each scenario.

**Exercise 2: AI Output Rewrite and Reflection**

*What to do:* Take an essay prompt from this course (or create a plausible one for CS357). Submit it to an LLM and save the output. Then rewrite the result from scratch, replacing the AI's perspective with your own — your actual examples, your actual reasoning, your actual conclusions. When you are done, write a short reflection: What did you change, and why? What did the AI miss about your perspective that you had to add?

*Starter hint:* Choose a prompt that asks for your opinion or your experience: "Describe a time when an AI tool surprised you with a mistake that mattered" or "Which alignment failure mode worries you most, and why?" Submit the prompt to an LLM and read its output critically. Notice what it cannot know: your specific experiences, your specific concerns, your specific values. Those are the gaps you fill in the rewrite.

*You've succeeded when:* You have the AI output, your rewritten version, and a 200-word reflection that specifically names what you changed, why the AI's version wasn't adequate, and what the rewriting process revealed about what you actually think.

**Exercise 3: Process-Based AI Rubric**

*What to do:* Design a grading rubric for a process-based AI assignment. The assignment asks students to use an LLM to help solve a problem, but grades them on their prompting strategy, their critical evaluation of the AI's responses, their decision to accept or reject AI suggestions, and the quality of their final revision — not just the final output itself.

*Starter hint:* Start with four criteria: (1) Prompt quality — does the prompt show the student understood the problem before asking? (2) Critical evaluation — did the student identify specific weaknesses in the AI's response and document them? (3) Acceptance/rejection decisions — did the student make explicit, reasoned decisions about which AI suggestions to keep and which to discard, with justifications? (4) Final synthesis — does the final work integrate the student's own reasoning in ways that go beyond the AI's output? For each criterion, write three performance levels: Excellent, Satisfactory, and Needs Improvement, with specific behavioral descriptions at each level.

*You've succeeded when:* Your rubric has at least 4 criteria, 3 performance levels each described with specific observable behaviors (not just "does well" vs. "does poorly"), and a brief justification for why each criterion captures genuine learning rather than just performance.

## Reflection Prompt

**Personal level:** Think about your own current relationship with AI tools in this course and others. Are you using them more as tutors (asking them to explain, pushing back on answers you don't understand, testing your own knowledge against them) or more as vending machines (getting the answer and moving on)? Be honest. What would you need to change about how you use AI to make it genuinely educational rather than a shortcut?

**Technical level:** You will graduate into a world where AI is a ubiquitous professional tool — present in writing, coding, analysis, design, and decision-making across nearly every field. What does "knowing how to do it yourself" mean in that world? Is there still value in building skills that AI can perform? If so, where does that value come from — economic utility, cognitive development, personal identity, or something else? And if not, what does education need to become?

**Societal level:** This course's AI use policies, your college's academic integrity framework, and national accreditation standards are all lagging behind the pace of AI capability development. Who should be responsible for keeping educational policy current? What would a well-governed, adaptive AI-in-education policy framework look like — one that doesn't freeze practices in 2024 or leave students and teachers without guidance for years at a time?
