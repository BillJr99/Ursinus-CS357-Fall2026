# Using AI for Academic Research

AI assistants are increasingly used in academic workflows — but they introduce new failure modes alongside new capabilities. **The core challenge**: language models generate fluent, authoritative-sounding text regardless of whether the underlying facts are correct. This activity builds a disciplined research workflow that uses AI for what it does well (synthesis, question generation, writing assistance) while maintaining human verification for factual claims.

We move from **the hallucination risk in research $\rightarrow$ a structured AI-assisted literature workflow $\rightarrow$ citation and fact-checking practices $\rightarrow$ the "Mathforum" Q&A approach for concept learning**.

## Key Concepts

| Term | Plain-English Definition | Research Example |
|------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| **Citation Hallucination** | A model invents a paper title, journal name, author, or DOI that sounds real but does not exist. | "Smith et al. (2021), *Transformer Attention for Scientific Discovery*, Nature AI, 3(4):12" — plausible format, may not exist |
| **Grounded Synthesis** | Summarizing only from provided source text, not from parametric memory — the same technique as RAG. | Pasting the abstract of a real paper and asking "Summarize the key findings in two sentences" |
| **Verification Pipeline** | A disciplined sequence: AI suggests → human checks → human cites. AI accelerates step 1; humans own steps 2–3. | Use Semantic Scholar or Google Scholar to confirm every paper suggested by the model |
| **Mathforum-Style Q&A** | A pedagogical approach: ask the AI to explain a concept, probe it with follow-up questions, then verify edge cases against primary sources. Named for the Math Forum's model of iterative questioning. | "Explain BPE tokenization." → "What happens with unknown characters?" → verify against Sennrich et al. (2016) |
| **Prompt for Uncertainty** | Explicitly asking the model to flag when it is unsure — reduces confident-sounding hallucination. | "Explain attention mechanisms. Flag any claim you are not confident about with [uncertain]." |
| **Literature Map** | A structured output showing how papers relate — connections, gaps, and open questions — generated by the AI from abstracts you provide. | Given 5 real abstracts, ask: "Draw a concept map of how these papers build on each other." |

# Part I: The Hallucination Risk in Research

## 1. Why Research Is a High-Hallucination Domain

In this Part you will understand why academic research is one of the most dangerous domains for AI hallucination, identify which research tasks AI handles reliably versus unreliably, and establish a personal verification habit.

**Why this matters:** A fabricated citation can derail your research before it starts. Citation hallucination (inventing plausible-sounding references) is one of the most documented failure modes of language models used for research. Unlike a factual error in casual conversation — which is embarrassing — a fabricated citation can be traced by your reader, destroying your credibility. On the other hand, AI is genuinely useful for concept synthesis, question generation, and drafting — tasks where you will verify the output anyway.

**Two kinds of research tasks.** *Fact-retrieval tasks* (What year was this paper published? Who co-authored this?) are high-risk: the model may confabulate specific bibliographic details. *Synthesis tasks* (Explain gradient descent in plain English; Generate five follow-up questions from this abstract) are lower-risk because you can evaluate the quality of the output directly, without a citation.

# Part II: A Structured AI-Assisted Literature Workflow

## 2. The Verification Pipeline

In this Part you will implement a five-step workflow that uses AI for synthesis and acceleration while keeping humans in control of verification and citation.

**The five-step pipeline:**

1. **Identify real seed papers** — Use Semantic Scholar, Google Scholar, or a course bibliography. Do not ask the AI to name papers; find them yourself from authoritative sources first.
2. **Paste and synthesize** — Give the AI real abstracts or excerpts. Ask it to summarize, find connections, and identify gaps. The model is now grounded in text you provided (RAG-style), not parametric memory.
3. **Generate follow-up questions** — Ask the AI to propose what a researcher in this area would want to know next. Use these as search queries.
4. **Verify and annotate** — For every factual claim in an AI summary, trace it to the source text you provided. If the claim is not in the text, mark it as unverified.
5. **Draft with AI, cite yourself** — Use the AI to draft prose, but insert every citation yourself from your verified list. Never let the AI write a citation.

## Code Cell

The code below demonstrates a grounded synthesis workflow using a local model and Chroma. You paste real paper abstracts; the model synthesizes only from what you provide.

```python
import requests
import chromadb

def embed(text):
    try:
        r = requests.post("http://localhost:11434/api/embeddings",
                          json={"model": "nomic-embed-text", "prompt": text}, timeout=120)
        return r.json()["embedding"]
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"[embed] {e}")
        return []

def chat(prompt):
    try:
        r = requests.post("http://localhost:11434/api/chat", json={
            "model": "llama3.2", "stream": False,
            "options": {"temperature": 0.0, "seed": 42},
            "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": prompt}]}, timeout=120)
        return r.json()["message"]["content"]
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"[chat] {e}")
        return ""

# Step 1: Paste real abstracts (user-supplied — these are illustrative placeholders).
# Replace with abstracts from papers you have found in Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar.
abstracts = {
    "vaswani2017": (
        "Vaswani et al. (2017). Attention Is All You Need. "
        "We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, "
        "dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely."
    ),
    "devlin2019": (
        "Devlin et al. (2019). BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding. "
        "We introduce a new language representation model called BERT, which stands for Bidirectional Encoder "
        "Representations from Transformers."
    ),
    "brown2020": (
        "Brown et al. (2020). Language Models are Few-Shot Learners. "
        "We demonstrate that scaling language models greatly improves task-agnostic, few-shot performance."
    ),
}

# Step 2: Index the abstracts
client = chromadb.Client()
col = client.create_collection("papers")
for key, text in abstracts.items():
    col.add(documents=[text], embeddings=[embed(text)], ids=[key])

# Step 3: Grounded synthesis
def research_query(question, k=2):
    hits = col.query(query_embeddings=[embed(question)], n_results=k)
    context = "\n\n".join(f"[{ids}] {doc}"
                          for ids, doc in zip(hits["ids"][0], hits["documents"][0]))
    prompt = (
        "You are a research assistant. Answer ONLY from the provided abstracts below. "
        "Cite the paper key in brackets. If the answer is not in the abstracts, say "
        "'Not found in provided sources — please search manually.'\n\n"
        f"Abstracts:\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"
    )
    return chat(prompt), context

answer, ctx = research_query("What architectural innovation did the Transformer introduce?")
print("RETRIEVED:\n", ctx, "\n\nSYNTHESIS:\n", answer)

# Step 4: Generate follow-up research questions
followup_prompt = (
    "Given these three paper abstracts about transformer models, generate five specific "
    "research questions a PhD student might investigate next. Focus on gaps these papers leave open.\n\n"
    + "\n\n".join(abstracts.values())
)
print("\nFOLLOW-UP QUESTIONS:\n", chat(followup_prompt))
```

# Part III: The Mathforum Q&A Approach

## 3. Iterative Concept Learning with AI

In this Part you will use the Mathforum-style iterative questioning technique for deep concept learning, and practice pairing AI explanation with primary-source verification.

**The Mathforum-style approach.** The Math Forum (now part of NCTM) modeled expert help as a dialogue: the helper asks clarifying questions and guides the student's thinking rather than simply providing answers. Applied to AI research assistance, this means:

1. **Prime the domain.** "I am learning about [topic]. Explain [concept] assuming I know [prerequisites]."
2. **Probe the edge cases.** "What happens when [boundary condition]? Does this always hold?"
3. **Request the limitation.** "What does this explanation leave out? Where does it break down?"
4. **Cross-check one specific claim.** Take *one* factual claim from the AI's explanation and verify it in a primary source (textbook, paper, Wikipedia with citations shown).
5. **Iterate.** Use what you found in step 4 to ask a sharper follow-up question.

This sequence is low-hallucination-risk because you are using AI for explanation (not citation) and you are verifying specific factual anchors yourself.

   - *You've succeeded when:* You have a table of 5 papers with a "verified / partially wrong / fabricated" label and a count of exactly-right, partially-wrong, and fabricated.

3. *Grounded literature map.* Find three real abstracts on any topic using Semantic Scholar. Index them with the code above. Ask the model to (a) identify the central argument of each, (b) find one connection between any two papers, and (c) identify one open question none of them answers.    - *What to do:* Run the grounded synthesis. For each of the three outputs (central arguments, connection, open question), read the source abstract and judge: does the model's claim appear in the text?

   - *You've succeeded when:* You have a table of five claims, their [uncertain] status, and your accuracy verdict, plus one sentence about whether flagging correlated with actual uncertainty.
